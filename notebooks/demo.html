Enroute is its own animal
Low‑bandwidth, high‑latency, moving — the unique technical considerations

Enroute links are small, contended, high‑latency (GEO), and always handing off. Making a service usable is a craft of its own — a sample:

BTU & terminal settings

Burst/timing and terminal parameters must match the network plan exactly, or the terminal never locks the carrier.

IP accelerators

WAN optimization and TCP/proxy acceleration to keep apps usable across a ½‑second GEO round trip and a thin pipe.

Packet tagging / QoS (DSCP)

Mark and queue so C2 and voice get the scarce bandwidth first; mis‑tagged bulk traffic starves the mission data.

Header / payload compression

Squeeze protocol overhead so the small pipe carries useful payload, not framing.

Dedicated routes / ports

Certain services or kits need specific routed paths or opened ports provisioned end‑to‑end — or they simply won't connect.

Latency‑aware app config

Disable chatty features, tune timeouts and retries for high‑latency / intermittent handoff so sessions survive a beam change.

✈️

Baseband airworthiness — not just the antenna. Both earn the Airworthiness Release (AWR). On USAF airframes (C‑17/C‑130) the Technical Airworthiness Authority (AFLCMC/EN‑EZ) assesses the config against MIL‑HDBK‑516C (per DAFI 62‑601) through the airframe SPO; on Army aircraft the Army airworthiness authority issues the AWR. Separately it needs ATTLA air‑transportability certification (MIL‑STD‑1791/209, USTRANSCOM DTR) — the letter in the cargo package. SOAR owns its rotary‑wing aircraft and a consolidated, specialized AWR process; the conventional force's is dispersed with joint dependencies. Doing this once, by default in NGC2 fielding removes the per‑DIV burden.

The resourcing strategy · get EMC to the warfighter at scale
Make EMC the default — then it can actually be plug‑and‑play for the warfighter

Not plug‑and‑play today — that friction costs the warfighter time. It can be, with two deliberate moves:

1 · DEFAULT‑ON
EMC‑ready by default

NGC2 & ESB‑E kits are EMC‑capable by default; non‑EMC configs go on a black list. Air‑/vessel‑worthiness is built into the requirement once.

2 · RESIDUAL = TRANSPORT
Transport is the enterprise's job

With EMC default‑on, the rest is transport — an enterprise problem owned by the Signal/Cyber forces that run the DODIN‑A and Army Data (DISA, ARCYBER, NETCOM, Data Ops, Signal Brigades) — not the division. That includes holding the recurring bandwidth accounts (Ka SIMOPS ≈ >$1M/yr; SpaceX ≈ $300K/yr) at echelon, not per‑DIV.

3 · RESULT
Plug‑and‑play for the DIV

From the division's seat, EMC becomes plug‑and‑play: bring the EMC‑ready kit, plug into enterprise‑owned transport. The complexity is owned at echelon, not gone.

The proposal · takeaway 2 · the best lever
Flip the triangle — make NGC2 EMC‑ready by default

The single highest‑payoff move. Today NGC2, ESB‑E, and EMC are separate basebands — built by the same vendors. Only a few are EMC‑capable, on a white list; everything else is an exception that costs friction and delay. Flip it: make the NGC2 baseband EMC‑capable and dual‑use by default, and put the few non‑EMC configs on a black list.

Today · white list

A few certified kits; every other mission is a one‑off. Friction, delay, low capacity.

Proposed · black list

Most kits ready out of the box; only exceptions are black‑listed. Speed, scale, low friction.

Converge the kit

T2COM / ASA(ALT) bake EMC airworthiness/vessel‑worthiness into the requirement and fielding — capability inherited, not re‑earned each mission.

USAF AMC oversight

The Airworthiness Release (AWR) for the C‑17/C‑130 belongs to the USAF airframe authority — AMC oversees it throughout; maritime adds the vessel‑worthiness authority. The Army can't self‑certify onto USAF airframes, so worthiness must be designed into the NGC2/ESB‑E requirement up front — and AMC's posture (e.g., C‑17 Starshield‑hatch) shapes what "default" means per platform.

Aperture employment strategy

Fixed‑fuselage Starshield C‑17 · PERSISTENT

Optimize robust availability of the C‑17 fleet fixed‑fuselage Starshield aperture as the high‑capacity backbone. Once installed, per‑mission setup is just the patch‑panel connection.

Starlink Mini AD HOC

Hold Mini for no‑notice / bridge links on any platform, accepting the bandwidth limits. The exception, not the plan.

Hatch‑mount AIRBORNE

Focus hatch on airborne/rapid units — GEO Ka/Ku on the C‑17, and Starshield on the C‑130/rotary. Removable, fast to swap, but pays the per‑mission install tax.

Force design · takeaway 3 · ready at scale
Regionally consolidate readiness, O&M, and expertise — ready for warfighters at scale

Consolidate EMC readiness, O&M, and expertise in Theater/Corps Signal units — EMC Air/Land/Sea, ready for warfighters at scale, affordably (As‑A‑Service). A company in each: 35th (18th ABN/WHC), 2nd (USAREUR‑AF), 160th (ARCENT), 516th (USARPAC/Alaska). The integration, logistics tail, config variants, APOD/SPOD relationships, and modernization are not a DIV additional duty — they belong with the Signal Brigades, Data Ops, and NETCOM. With EMC‑by‑default kits at the DIV, the mission works — airborne assets stay the spearhead.

Theater / Corps Signal unit · EMC Company

One company consolidates the readiness, O&M, and expertise: the airworthiness (AWR)/vessel‑worthiness packages, the AMC / SATCOM / Starshield / DISA / Data Ops / ARCYBER‑NETCOM RCC relationships, the pre‑staged certified kits, and a current operator bench — working familiar APODs/SPODs with habitual partners. It owns the routing considerations beyond RHN/GAIT, BTU settings, and RCC dependencies, and feeds modernization. EMC as‑a‑service to the warfighter.

Proof of the model · the AWR process
SOAR — consolidated · fast

Owns its rotary‑wing aircraft and runs specialized units just for AWR. The process and expertise are concentrated in one place — so airworthiness moves fast.

Conventional — dispersed · slow

Rides USAF airframes (C‑17/C‑130): the AWR runs through the USAF airframe authority, AMC, and ATTLA — joint dependencies, distributed across commands. Far slower, far more complex.

The lesson: SOAR is fast because it concentrates the process and expertise. The conventional force can't own the airframes — but it can concentrate the EMC AWR, integration, and expertise the same way: in Theater/Corps Signal units.
