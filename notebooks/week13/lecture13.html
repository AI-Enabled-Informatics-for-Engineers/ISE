🏛 1 — Responsible AI: Harms, Governance, and the NIST AI RMF

Responsible AI is not a set of ethical aspirations. It is a set of engineering requirements. A system that produces biased outputs, fails to explain its decisions, or causes demonstrable harm to identifiable groups is not a responsible AI system — regardless of its accuracy on benchmark evaluations.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides a practical governance structure organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. These are not sequential phases — they are concurrent disciplines that operate continuously throughout the AI system lifecycle.

Govern
Establish policies and accountability
Define who is responsible for AI risk. Establish policies for responsible development and deployment. Create organizational processes for oversight.
Map
Identify risks in context
Identify who uses the system, who is affected by it, and what harms could result. Understand the deployment context: what decisions does the system inform?
Measure
Quantify and evaluate risks
Implement evaluation metrics for fairness, robustness, privacy, and safety. This is where Huyen Ch. 4's evaluation framework applies — extended to responsible AI dimensions.
Manage
Treat, monitor, and respond
Apply mitigations, monitor deployed systems, and respond to incidents. Includes documentation, transparency reports, and stakeholder communication.

Connecting to Huyen Chapter 4 — Evaluation as Responsible AI Infrastructure

Huyen's Chapter 4 frames evaluation as a technical problem: how do you measure whether a model is doing what you want? Week 13 extends this framing. The evaluation infrastructure Huyen describes — held-out test sets, multiple metrics, sliced evaluation across subgroups — is exactly the Measure function of the NIST AI RMF.

Sliced evaluation is the critical technique for responsible AI measurement. You do not just measure average performance — you measure performance broken down by demographic group, by use case type, by input complexity, by geographic region. A model with 92% overall accuracy and 61% accuracy for a protected subgroup is not a responsible AI system, even if the overall number looks good. Sliced evaluation surfaces the gap that average metrics hide.

The responsible AI audit checklist — minimum viable for production deployment: (1) Fairness evaluation across relevant subgroups with documented thresholds. (2) Transparency documentation: what the model does, what it cannot do, and what data it was trained on. (3) Human oversight mechanism for high-stakes decisions. (4) Incident response plan: what happens when the model fails. (5) Regular re-evaluation schedule as deployment conditions change.

⚖️ 2 — Bias, Fairness, and What Huyen's Evaluation Framework Misses

Huyen's evaluation framework is technically rigorous. It is also incomplete for responsible AI deployment, because it optimizes for predictive accuracy on a distribution — not for equitable outcomes across subgroups. Bias and fairness require a different evaluation lens.

Sources of Bias in AI Systems

Source 1
Training Data Bias
Historical data encodes historical inequities. A hiring model trained on past hiring decisions learns patterns that reflect past discrimination. Even without protected attributes in the input, proxies (zip code, university attended) recreate the bias.
Source 2
Label Bias
Labels assigned by human annotators reflect annotator demographics, incentives, and biases. If annotators from one background label content from another background, systematic errors can masquerade as ground truth.
Source 3
Feedback Loop Bias
Model predictions influence future data collection. A recommendation system that shows certain content more often to certain groups generates biased training data for the next version. This is a causal structure problem — exactly Week 12's territory.
Source 4
Deployment Context Mismatch
A model performs equally well across groups in evaluation but is deployed in a context where one group interacts with it very differently. The distributional shift creates performance disparities that were invisible in evaluation.

Fairness Metrics — Selecting the Right One

There is no single fairness metric that is correct in all contexts. The right metric depends on the deployment decision and what kind of equity you are trying to achieve.

Fairness CriterionDefinitionBest For
Demographic ParityEqual positive prediction rate across groupsHigh-stakes allocation (loans, hiring) where representation matters
Equalized OddsEqual TPR and FPR across groupsBinary classification where both errors matter equally
Equal OpportunityEqual TPR (recall) across groupsWhen false negatives are the primary harm (disease screening)
Predictive ParityEqual PPV (precision) across groupsWhen false positives are the primary harm (recidivism prediction)
Individual FairnessSimilar individuals receive similar predictionsWhen group membership is less relevant than individual similarity
Critical limitation: Multiple fairness criteria are mathematically incompatible when base rates differ across groups (Chouldechova, 2017). You cannot simultaneously satisfy demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity in a real system with different class prevalences. Selecting a fairness criterion is a normative decision, not a technical one — it requires engagement with affected communities, not just engineers.

🎯 3 — Threat Modeling for AI Systems — STRIDE + OWASP

Threat modeling is the practice of systematically identifying what can go wrong in a system before it goes wrong. For AI systems, threat modeling has to address both the traditional software security threats and the AI-specific threats documented in the OWASP LLM Top 10.

The STRIDE framework provides a structured vocabulary for threats. Applied to an AI system, each STRIDE category maps to specific AI attack vectors.

S
Spoofing
Impersonating a legitimate identity to gain unauthorized access or trust.
AI context: model or API endpoint impersonation; prompt injection mimicking system instructions
T
Tampering
Unauthorized modification of data, model weights, or system behavior.
AI context: training data poisoning (OWASP LLM03); model weight modification; RAG index injection
R
Repudiation
Denying that an action was taken; absence of audit evidence.
AI context: no audit log of model decisions or agent actions (directly addressed by Week 11 audit log pattern)
I
Information Disclosure
Exposing sensitive information to unauthorized parties.
AI context: training data memorization; model inversion; sensitive info in context window (OWASP LLM06)
D
Denial of Service
Making a system unavailable by overwhelming its resources.
AI context: adversarial prompt flooding; recursive agent loops; expensive generation attacks (OWASP LLM04)
E
Elevation of Privilege
Gaining access beyond what is authorized.
AI context: prompt injection bypassing safety filters; jailbreaking; agent tool privilege escalation (OWASP LLM08)

The Threat Modeling Process — Four Steps

1
Define the system and its assets

Draw the system boundary. Identify assets: model weights, training data, user data, API keys, inference outputs, audit logs. An asset is anything of value that a threat actor would want to steal, corrupt, or deny access to.

2
Identify threat actors and their capabilities

Who would attack your system? External adversaries (prompt injection, jailbreaking), malicious insiders (data exfiltration), competitors (model extraction), regulators (compliance failures). Each actor has different motivations and capabilities.

3
Enumerate threats using STRIDE × OWASP

For each asset, apply the STRIDE categories. Cross-reference with the OWASP LLM Top 10 for AI-specific threats. Document the attack vector, the asset at risk, and the potential impact. See the Threat Modeling tab for the full reference.

4
Define controls and mitigations

For each threat, identify the control: prevent (make the attack impossible), detect (know when it happens), respond (contain damage when detected). Map controls to the NIST AI RMF Manage function. This mapping becomes the M6 mitigation and audit plan.

The M6 deliverable is this threat model. Your threat model should cover: the three most critical assets in your system, at least five threats mapped to STRIDE categories and OWASP risks, and a control for each threat. The Threat Modeling tab on this page provides the full reference template and the OWASP-to-STRIDE mapping table.

🔑 4 — Cryptography Fundamentals for AI Engineers

You do not need to implement cryptography. You need to understand it well enough to make correct architectural decisions about data protection, key management, and secure communication in AI systems. The goal is informed use of cryptographic primitives — not implementation.

Symmetric Encryption — One Key, High Speed

Symmetric encryption uses the same key to encrypt and decrypt. It is computationally efficient and appropriate for large data volumes — bulk data at rest, large context windows, training data stores. The dominant standard is AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) with 256-bit keys. AES-256 in GCM mode provides both encryption and authentication (authenticated encryption), which is what NIST SP 800-57 recommends for data at rest in AI systems.

The critical constraint: both parties must share the key securely. Key distribution is the hard problem in symmetric cryptography. You cannot send the symmetric key over an unencrypted channel — which is where asymmetric cryptography becomes necessary.

Asymmetric Encryption — Key Pairs, Secure Exchange

Asymmetric encryption uses mathematically linked key pairs: a public key (freely distributable) and a private key (never shared). Data encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the corresponding private key. RSA-2048 and NIST-approved elliptic curve algorithms (ECDH, ECDSA) are the current standards.

The primary use case for asymmetric encryption in AI systems is key exchange — using asymmetric cryptography to securely establish a shared symmetric key, then using symmetric encryption for the actual data. This is the TLS handshake: asymmetric for authentication and key exchange, symmetric for the bulk data stream. Your API calls to model providers, your user data in transit, your audit log exports — all use this pattern.

NIST SP 800-57 — Key Management Lifecycle

NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 provides the authoritative guidance on cryptographic key management. For AI engineers, the critical concepts are the key lifecycle states and the key management requirements that apply at each state.

Lifecycle StateDescriptionAI System Application
Pre-activationKey generated, not yet in useGenerate encryption keys before provisioning AI infrastructure; store securely in a key management service (KMS)
ActiveKey in use for protection and recoveryEncrypt training data, model artifacts, API secrets, user PII; rotate on defined schedule
DeactivatedNo longer used to protect new data; still needed for existing dataAfter key rotation: retain for decrypting existing encrypted training data; do not use for new operations
CompromisedKey suspected or confirmed compromisedImmediately revoke; re-encrypt all data protected with the compromised key; incident response
DestroyedKey permanently deletedAfter retention period; must cryptographically verify destruction; document in audit log
The four key management requirements every AI system must satisfy (NIST SP 800-57): (1) Keys must be generated by approved algorithms with sufficient entropy. (2) Keys must be stored in a protected key store — not hardcoded in source code, not in environment variables that end up in logs, not in a spreadsheet. (3) Keys must be rotated on a defined schedule — typically annually for long-term keys, more frequently for session keys. (4) Key use must be logged — every encryption and decryption operation associated with an audit entry.

🔒 5 — Data Protection in AI Pipelines

An AI system handles sensitive data at every stage of its lifecycle: training data collection and storage, model training (gradient exposure), inference (user queries and context), and output logging. Each stage requires specific protection measures.

Data at Rest

Training data, model weights, fine-tuning datasets, and audit logs must be encrypted at rest using AES-256 (or equivalent). Cloud provider managed encryption (AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault, Google Cloud KMS) provides the correct implementation for most AI engineering contexts. The key management service handles key generation, rotation, and access control — your application never handles raw key material.

Data in Transit

All API calls to model providers, all user queries, all retrieved context (RAG pipeline), and all model outputs must traverse TLS 1.2 or higher. Do not use HTTP for any component of an AI pipeline that handles user data. Validate TLS certificates — do not disable certificate verification even in development, because that habit will end up in production code.

Data in Context — The Unique AI Problem

The RAG context window creates a data protection problem that traditional software does not have: user data, retrieved documents, and system instructions all exist together in the prompt. If the context window is logged (as it should be, for debugging and audit), the log contains potentially sensitive information from multiple sources simultaneously.

The context window security requirement: Audit logs of context windows must be treated as sensitive data — encrypted at rest, access-controlled, and subject to the same retention policies as the most sensitive data they contain. A context window log containing PII is PII data and must be protected as such. This is frequently overlooked in AI system design and is a direct OWASP LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) risk.

Model Weight Protection

Fine-tuned model weights are a high-value asset — they represent significant investment and may encode proprietary data. Model weights should be: encrypted at rest, access-controlled with least-privilege principles, versioned with integrity checksums, and subject to change management processes. A tampered model weight is the Training Data Poisoning threat (OWASP LLM03) post-training — an adversary who can modify weights can modify model behavior arbitrarily.

🏗 6 — Putting It Together: The Trust & Security Package (M6)

M6 requires you to produce a Trust & Security Package for your course project. It has three components: a threat model, a logging and audit plan, and a responsible AI notes document. These are not separate documents — they are three views of the same underlying analysis.

1
Threat Model

Assets identified and prioritized. Threats enumerated using STRIDE with OWASP LLM Top 10 cross-reference. Controls mapped to each threat (prevent/detect/respond). Risk level assigned to each threat (High/Medium/Low). This is the security engineering view of your system.

2
Logging and Audit Plan

What is logged, where, in what format, with what retention period, and with what access controls. Minimum log events: model inputs and outputs (sanitized), gate decisions (from M6 Week 11 work), authentication events, data access events, key usage events. This is the accountability view of your system.

3
Responsible AI Notes

Harms inventory: what could go wrong for users, third parties, and society. Bias evaluation: what subgroups exist in your system's user base and how will you measure differential performance. Transparency: what will you tell users about how the system works and what it cannot do. Human oversight: what decisions require human review. This is the governance view of your system.

The M6 insight: These three components are not independent — they inform each other. A threat in the threat model may require a new log event in the audit plan. A harm in the responsible AI notes may require a new control in the threat model. A fairness evaluation gap may surface a bias-amplification threat. Build all three components iteratively, not sequentially.
