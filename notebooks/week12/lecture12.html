📖 Week 12 Lecture

Causal Informatics: Interventions & Decision Impact

Six sections. The first half establishes why prediction and causation are different. The second half provides the tools and connects them to your AI engineering coursework.

🔄 1 — The Core Distinction: P(Y|X) vs. P(Y|do(X))

Every model you have built in this course answers the same fundamental question: given this input, what output should I predict? That question has a formal name: conditional probability — P(Y given X). The model learns from historical data where X and Y co-occurred, and it uses that learned association to make predictions on new inputs.

Conditional probability is extraordinarily useful for prediction tasks. But when you want to use a prediction to make a decision — to take an action — prediction is not sufficient. You need to know what happens if you intervene — if you actively set X to a specific value. That question is P(Y|do(X)) — the probability of Y if you forcibly set X, regardless of what naturally determines X in the world.

These two quantities are different. Sometimes radically different. The gap between them is the source of most bad AI-driven decisions.

The ice cream example: Ice cream sales predict drowning rates extremely well. A model trained on this data will assign high P(drowning | high ice cream sales). If you use this to decide whether to ban ice cream, you are asking P(drowning | do(ice_cream=low)) — which is a completely different question. The causal structure is: summer heat causes both ice cream consumption and swimming; swimming causes drowning. Intervening on ice cream sales changes nothing about drowning rates. The conditional is real. The causal claim is false.

Why Accurate Models Can Drive Wrong Decisions

A model can have excellent predictive accuracy — low loss, high precision, strong benchmark scores — and still produce bad decisions when used to guide interventions. This is not a model quality problem. It is a question-type mismatch: the model was trained to answer a prediction question, and you are asking it a causal question. Getting the right answer to the wrong question is still the wrong answer.

This is the connection to Huyen's Chapters 3 and 4. Data collected under natural (observational) conditions reflects the correlations in the world — including spurious correlations that predict well but causally mean nothing. Evaluation metrics that reward predictive accuracy reward the model for learning all correlations, causal and spurious alike. High evaluation scores do not guarantee that the model's predictions can be safely used for decisions.

🗺 2 — The Causal Framework: DAGs and Confounders

The causal inference framework gives us a precise language for reasoning about what causes what and how interventions work. The central tool is the Directed Acyclic Graph — DAG. A DAG is a graph where nodes are variables and edges represent causal relationships: an arrow from A to B means A causally influences B.

Confounders — The Source of Most Problems

A confounder is a variable that causes both the treatment (your intervention variable) and the outcome. In the ice cream example, summer heat is a confounder: it causes ice cream sales (the treatment) and it causes drowning (the outcome, through swimming). When you observe the correlation between ice cream and drowning, you are picking up the shared influence of the confounder, not a direct causal link.

Confounders corrupt observational inference because the observational data contains both the causal signal you want and the confounding signal you need to remove. A model trained on confounded data learns both signals simultaneously — and often learns the confounding signal more strongly, because it explains more variance.

Causal DAG — Confounder Structure

Orange = confounder, Green = outcome, dashed red = spurious correlation (observed but not causal). The confounder causes both T and Y, creating a back-door path that makes T appear to cause Y.

The Do-Operator — Surgical Intervention

Judea Pearl's do-operator formalizes what it means to intervene. When you condition on X — P(Y|X=x) — you are asking: in the subset of observations where X happened to equal x, what is Y? This includes all the natural causes of X, including confounders.

When you intervene on X — P(Y|do(X=x)) — you are asking: if I surgically set X to x, cutting all its natural causes and holding it there by force, what is Y? This removes the confounding paths. The do-operator erases all incoming arrows to the intervened variable in the DAG.

The adjustment formula (back-door criterion): If you can identify the set of confounders C that blocks all back-door paths from treatment to outcome, then P(Y|do(T)) = Σc P(Y|T,C=c) · P(C=c). This is adjustment — you compute the causal effect by averaging the conditional effect over the distribution of the confounder. DoWhy automates this calculation.

🔧 3 — DoWhy: Causal Reasoning in Practice

DoWhy (pywhy.org) is the Microsoft Research library for causal inference in Python. It provides a four-step workflow that maps directly onto the causal framework from Section 2.

1
Model — Define the causal DAG

Specify the causal graph as a list of edges: which variables cause which. This encodes your domain knowledge about the causal structure. The model is your causal assumptions made explicit.

2
Identify — Find an identification strategy

DoWhy searches the DAG for a valid identification strategy — a way to express P(Y|do(T)) in terms of observational quantities. It may find back-door adjustment, front-door adjustment, or instrumental variables, depending on the graph structure.

3
Estimate — Compute the causal effect

Apply the identification strategy to actual data. DoWhy supports multiple estimators: linear regression adjustment, propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, and doubly-robust estimation.

4
Refute — Test the robustness of the estimate

Run refutation tests: add a random common cause (should not change the estimate), replace the treatment with a placebo (estimate should go to zero), use a subset of the data (estimate should be stable). If your estimate fails these tests, your causal assumptions may be wrong.

Why the refutation step matters: The three prior steps are only as good as your DAG assumptions. Refutation tests are the discipline of checking whether your causal conclusions are robust to violations of those assumptions. A causal estimate that survives placebo treatment and random common cause tests is much more credible than one that does not.

The Colab notebook for this week walks you through all four steps on a procurement scenario: does supplier lead time (treatment) causally affect defect rate (outcome), controlling for supplier tier (confounder)?

📚 4 — Connecting to AI Engineering (Ch. 3 + Ch. 4)

Huyen does not use the term "causal inference" in Chapters 3 and 4, but the causal reasoning problems are everywhere in both chapters. Reading them with causal eyes reveals how much of what Huyen calls data quality and evaluation quality problems are really confounding problems.

Chapter 3: Data — Where Confounders Enter

Huyen discusses data collection bias: training data that over-represents certain conditions will produce models that underperform on under-represented conditions. In causal terms, data collection process is a confounder. If your training data was collected when the system was operated by experienced users, user experience confounds the model's apparent performance — it is not learning the true relationship between inputs and outputs, it is learning the relationship conditioned on experienced-user behavior. Deployed to novice users, performance degrades not because the model is wrong, but because the confounder changed.

Chapter 4: Evaluation — Where Spurious Correlations Mislead

Huyen's evaluation framework rewards predictive accuracy on a held-out set. But if the held-out set is drawn from the same confounded distribution as the training set, a model can achieve high evaluation scores by learning spurious correlations rather than causal mechanisms. The model that scores 91% on the held-out evaluation and 54% on production (the pharma RAG example from Week 10) is exactly this phenomenon: excellent at predicting the evaluation distribution, poor at generalizing to the deployment distribution — which has different confounding structure.

The practical implication: Whenever you are using a model's predictions to drive decisions — not just reporting predictions, but acting on them — you should ask: is this a prediction task or a causal task? If acting on the prediction changes the environment in ways the model was not trained on, you are in causal territory. The model's prediction accuracy does not tell you whether the action will work.

🏭 5 — ISE Applications: Causality in Engineering Practice

Industrial and systems engineering contexts are particularly rich with causal questions. This section maps the causal framework onto the domains ISE students encounter most.

Application 1
Predictive Maintenance
Sensor X predicts failure. Should you intervene on sensor X? Only if X is causal. If X is a symptom (caused by the underlying failure mode) rather than a cause, intervening on X changes the reading but not the failure rate. DoWhy helps you distinguish symptom from cause using DAG structure.
Application 2
Process Improvement
Shift A has 3× better quality than Shift B. Is the shift supervisor causing quality differences? Or are other factors confounded — different machine sets, different raw material batches, different product mix? Answering this requires causal analysis, not correlation analysis.
Application 3
Supply Chain Decisions
Supplier lead time correlates with defect rate in your data. Should you switch to a different supplier tier? The correlation may be confounded by product complexity — complex products take longer and have more defects. Adjusting for complexity may eliminate the lead time effect entirely.
Application 4
A/B Testing
Why does random assignment work? Because randomization makes treatment independent of all confounders — it breaks the back-door paths in the DAG. An A/B test is the experimental equivalent of do(T): you forcibly assign treatment, removing the natural causes of who would have received it.
The ISE connection to Six Sigma: The "C" in DMAIC — Control — implicitly requires causal understanding. Controlling a process means knowing which input variables causally drive output quality. A correlation-based model can tell you which variables are associated with quality, but only a causal model can tell you which ones to control and how. DoWhy makes explicit what experienced engineers have always known intuitively: you need to understand the mechanism, not just the association.

💬 6 — When Is Prediction Not Enough?

This is your Canvas discussion question, and it is the synthesis question for Week 12.

Prediction is sufficient when you are reporting on a fixed environment — forecasting demand, flagging anomalies for human review, generating a summary. In these cases, you are not acting on the environment; you are observing it. The conditional probability P(Y|X) is exactly what you need.

Prediction is insufficient when your action changes the environment in ways the model did not see. When you recommend a drug, the patient takes it and their physiology changes. When you change a supplier, your supply chain dynamics change. When you adjust a process parameter, downstream processes react. In all these cases, you are asking P(Y|do(T)) and your model only knows P(Y|T). The gap between those two quantities is exactly how much your decision could go wrong.

The cleanest test: does your action break any of the causal arrows in the data-generating process that your model was trained on? If yes, you are in causal territory and you need causal tools.

Demo 2 — The Do-Operator

See how conditioning and intervening produce different quantities from the same data — and why only the causal quantity supports valid decisions.

Prediction vs. Intervention — Formal Notation

P(Y | T=high) — conditional: "among cases where T was high, what is Y?"
Includes all confounding paths. Valid for prediction. Not valid for intervention decisions.
P(Y | do(T=high)) — interventional: "if I force T to high, what is Y?"
Cuts all incoming arrows to T. Removes confounding. Valid for decisions.
P(Y|T) ≠ P(Y|do(T)) whenever there exists a confounder C with C→T and C→Y

Back-door adjustment formula (Pearl, 2009):

P(Y|do(T)) = Σc P(Y|T, C=c) · P(C=c)

Average the conditional effect of T on Y over the distribution of confounders C. DoWhy's identify_effect() and estimate_effect() automate this calculation.
